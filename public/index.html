Four steps, every time you dial

No settings to tune before you're useful with this. Pick someone to call, and go.

01

Pick who picks up

Choose a built-in prospect — a rushed office manager, a numbers-only CFO, a gatekeeper who's heard every opener — or build your own with a personality brief that drives exactly how they'll react.

02

Make the call, out loud

No script window, no typing your pitch in advance. You talk like you would on a real call — filler words, false starts, and all.

03

They push back for real

Objections, interruptions, a hang-up if you lose them. Patience runs down when you're vague or pushy, and back up when you're not — the call can end badly, and often will.

04

Get the debrief

A scored breakdown of your opener, your objection handling, and your close — with the exact line where the call turned, saved to your history so you can watch the trend.

Why saying it out loud is the point

Reading a script silently and saying your pitch out loud, under mild pressure, use different muscles. The gap between "I know what I'd say" and "I said it, clearly, while someone was pushing back" is where most cold-calling skill actually lives.

That gap is hard to close on real prospects, because every failed rep is a burned contact. Dial-In gives you the repetition without the cost — a hang-up here just means you try the same objection again in thirty seconds.

It's built to be uncomfortable in the same specific way a real cold call is uncomfortable: you don't know exactly what's coming next, and you can't take it back once you've said it.

Low patience, high stakes. Every prospect starts with a patience level tied to their role — a busy office manager gives you more slack than a CFO who's heard every pitch this quarter.
Nothing is scripted for you. The prospect's replies come from their personality brief and how the call is going, not a fixed decision tree.
The debrief is specific. "Work on your close" isn't useful. "You never actually asked for the meeting after minute two" is.
